# Postmodern Eclecticism
[Home](README.md)

## What it is

Postmodern Eclecticism developed as part of the broader Postmodern movement that became influential during the 1970s and 1980s. Instead of following one strict visual system, Postmodern designers combined different historical references, decorative elements, colors, materials, and popular-culture influences.

The style challenged Modernism's emphasis on simplicity, clarity, and functional design. Postmodern designers often embraced complexity, contradiction, decoration, humor, and references to earlier styles. This made designs more expressive and allowed elements from different periods and visual traditions to appear together.

## Recognize and apply it

Three visual features that can help identify Postmodern Eclecticism are:

1. **Mixing different styles and references** — Designs can combine historical forms, contemporary imagery, popular culture, and decorative elements rather than following one visual style.
2. **Bold color and decoration** — Bright colors, patterns, ornament, and unusual surface treatments are often used to make the design more expressive.
3. **Playful and unexpected composition** — Designers may use unusual combinations, exaggeration, asymmetry, and visual contrast instead of the restrained organization associated with Modernism.

The style works well when a design should feel expressive, memorable, playful, or visually surprising. In a hero design, these features could be used while keeping the headline and CTA readable.

## Historical examples

### Example 1: Proust Armchair, 1978

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** Alessandro Mendini, *Proust Armchair*, 1978, represented in the Victoria and Albert Museum's discussion of Postmodern design.
- **Direct collection/source link:** [V&A — What is Postmodernism?](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-is-postmodernism)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View Postmodernism at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-is-postmodernism). Follow the V&A's image-use and copyright guidance before reproducing museum images.
- **What I observe:** The chair combines an older decorative furniture form with a highly expressive painted surface. The combination makes the object look intentionally decorative and visually unexpected rather than minimal.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would combine a familiar structured layout with an unexpected decorative pattern or historical visual reference while keeping the headline and CTA easy to read.

### Example 2: Casablanca and Carlton Furniture Designs, 1981

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** Ettore Sottsass, *Casablanca and Carlton Furniture Designs*, 1981, Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A).
- **Direct collection/source link:** [V&A — Postmodern Design](https://www.vam.ac.uk/info/collection-selection-boxes-postmodern-design)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View the designs at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/info/collection-selection-boxes-postmodern-design). Credit: © Victoria and Albert Museum, London. Follow the V&A's rights guidance before reproducing the image.
- **What I observe:** The designs use bright colors, geometric shapes, unusual angles, and decorative elements. Familiar furniture forms are changed through unexpected combinations that make the objects appear playful and unconventional.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would use contrasting geometric shapes, bold color blocks, and an asymmetrical composition around the product while placing the CTA in a quieter area so it remains readable.
